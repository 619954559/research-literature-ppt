Abstract
Background
Vertebral artery occlusive disease is a leading cause of posterior circulation ischemic stroke. Endovascular recanalization has been described as a viable treatment option for individuals with symptomatic vertebral artery occlusion who fail to respond to appropriate medicinal therapy. However, endovascular recanalization of symptomatic long-segment nonacute vertebral artery blockage is associated with significant surgical risk and the possibility of perioperative complications.
Case presentation
Despite rigorous pharmacologic therapy, a patient with a protracted segmental left vertebral artery blockage continues to experience cerebral hypoperfusion and neurologic impairments. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed a stump at the left vertebral artery ostium of segment V1, the right V4 segment was obviously congenitally thin, and a small amount of reflux was observed at the distal end of the left V4 segment. The posterior communicating artery was closed. We considered the diagnosis of left vertebral artery blockage (near V1 to V4). The patient underwent balloon dilatation of the occlusion and stenting. Finally, revascularization was successfully performed for long-segment occlusion of the left vertebral artery. DSA indicated complete segmental filling 3 months and 2 years after the patient received medication, and there were no new neurologic complaints throughout the 2-year follow-up period.
Conclusion
After detailed evaluation and screening, recanalization of long-segment vertebral artery occlusion in the nonacute phase is feasible.

Background
Vertebral artery (VA) occlusive disease is the leading cause of posterior circulation ischemic events, accounting for 20–32% of transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) or ischemic strokes [1, 2]. Atherosclerosis is the most common cause of this condition [3]. Currently, symptomatic occlusion occurring 24 h after onset is classified as nonacute phase occlusion. Approximately 80% of patients with symptomatic nonacute VA occlusions have a poor prognosis, and the mortality rate can still reach 40%, even with rigorous pharmacologic therapy [4, 5]. Current clinical studies have indicated that surgery is not often performed under these circumstances because of its complexity and serious consequences [6, 7]. Most recently, partial segmental endovascular recanalization has been reported as a feasible treatment for VA occlusion, with a high success rate of 86.0–91.3% and a low rate of periprocedural complications of 4.3–12.0% [7, 8]. However, reports of successful revascularization of long-segment nonacute VA occlusions (near V1 to V4) are rare. Here, we present the case of a patient with long-segment nonacute left VA occlusion (near V1 to V4) who successfully underwent one-stage completion of endovascular recanalization.
Case presentation
Medcial history
A 59-year-old man with hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia was referred to our hospital. The patient had bad speech, choked on drinking water, numbness and reduced mobility of the left side of the leg, numbness of the right limb and vertigo without provocation for 20 days. Following the onset of these symptoms, the patient was first taken to a local hospital and underwent magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head, which revealed a fresh infarct in the left medulla oblongata (Fig. 1A). The patient’s medical history was as follows: he denied a history of tobacco, alcohol, and chronic medical illnesses such as heart disease, as well as a family or genetic history of cerebral infarction and any history of prior endovascular therapy. Five days after the first episode, the patient received optimal medical therapy including dual antiplatelet agents and risk factor modifications. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed occlusion of segments V3 and V4 of the left VA (Fig. 1B). Magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) revealed possible occlusion of the intracranial segment of the left VA (Fig. 1C). Therefore, despite intensive drug therapy, the patient continued to have neurological deficits, the patient experience longer episodes of vertigo, more frequent attacks, and worse symptoms compared to before. The further evaluation of endovascular treatment was needed. The preoperative evaluation results were shown below. His blood pressure was 130/100 mmHg. The left limb manual muscle testing was IV+, with numbness. The right limb manual muscle testing was V. The muscle tone of the left and right limbs was normal. In the left and right finger-nose test, the heel-knee-tibia test were not stable or accurate. The left and right alternations were clumsy. The left and right rebound tests were positive. The Laboratory test results were as follows: GLU, 7.16 mmol/L; TC, 3.41 mmol/L; TG, 3.26 mmol/L; HDL-C, 0.73 mmol/L; LDL-C, 1.54 mmol/L; and HCY, 8.6 mmol/L. Arterial spin labelling (ASL) revealed regional hypoperfusion in the left cerebellar hemisphere (Fig. 1D). Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) was performed on the patient. Aortic arch angiography revealed a visible stump at the left VA ostium of segment V1 (Fig. 1E). Right subclavian arteriography revealed that the terminal part of the right VA V4 segment was clearly congenitally thin, with a small amount of reflux observed in the distal part of the V4 segment on the left side. The basilar artery and its branches were clearly filled and developed. The Allcock test indicated that the posterior communicating artery was not open (Fig. 1F, G, H, I). High-resolution magnetic resonance vessel wall imaging (HRMR-VWI) revealed occlusion of segments V3 and V4 of the left VA with a soft plaque nature (Fig. 1J, K, L). At 1 month, we decided to perform endovascular intervention to recanalize the left VA. The patient was given dual antiplatelet medications (aspirin 100 mg/day and clopidogrel 75 mg/day) for 7 days before the surgery.

Surgical procedure
Patient was placed in the supine position. The surgery was performed under general anaesthesia. The Seldinger technique was used to acquire percutaneous access to the left femoral artery (FA) before a 6 F introducer sheath was inserted and the right FA before an 8 F introducer sheath was inserted. The patient was intra-arterially heparinized. A 5 F VER catheter (Cordis, New Jersey, USA) was delivered to the right subclavian artery (SA) via the left vascular sheath, and a 6 F Neuron MAX long sheath (Penumbra, Alameda, USA) was delivered to the left SA proximal to the VA ostium via the right vascular sheath under the guidance of a 0.035-in loach guidewire (Terumo, Tokyo, Japan). After the loach guidewire was retracted, the Synchro 0.014-in microguidewire (Stryker, Michigan, USA) guided the Excelsior SL-10 microcatheter (Stryker, Michigan, USA) into the 6 F Neuron MAX long sheath. The Synchro was applied to carefully pass through the left SA proximal to the VA ostium, gradually probing the lumen of the vessel distally and following up with the microcatheter. However, it did not pass through the occluded vessel lumen when it reached the C2 level.
As a result, the exchange of the PT 0.014-in microguidewire (Abbott, California, USA) continued to explore the lumen of the vessel towards the distal end of the process, revealing that it entered into the vascular dissection of segment V2. The microguidewire and microcatheter continued to be placed ahead, and at the end of the occlusion, the microguidewire and microcatheter could not enter the vessel. The 5 F VER catheter from the right SA were used to perform the road map. After several attempts, the microguidewire entered the blood vessel, followed by the microcatheter, and then retracted. Microcatheter angiography revealed that it was located in the actual lumen of the distal V4 segment (Fig. 2A). The Synchro microguidewire was delivered to the P2 segment of the left posterior cerebral artery, followed by the microcatheter, which was exchanged for the Transend microguidewire (Stryker, Michigan, USA).

A PTCA 2.0 mm×20 mm balloon (JWMS, Weihai, China) was introduced and gradually dilated from the V4 segment occlusion site to the proximal end. After the balloon was retracted, the intermediate 6 F catheter (Navien) was introduced into the distal section of V4, and negative pressure was suctioned at the same time. We retracted the Navien device, but no visible thrombus was aspirated, and normal blood flow did not return to the vessel lumen. Under the guidance of an angiogram, a 2.5 mm×36 mm rapamycin drug-coated coronary stent (JWMS, Weihai, China) was implanted in occluded segment V4. An Apollo 3.5 mm×23 mm balloon-expanded stent (Microport Medical, Shanghai, China) and an Apollo 3.5 mm×13 mm balloon-expanded stent (Microport Medical, Shanghai, China) were consecutively implanted at the junction of the V4 and V3 segments and at the stenosis of the V3 segment. A 3.5 mm×36 mm rapamycin drug-coated coronary stent (JWMS, Weihai, China) was implanted at the site of vascular dissection in segment V2.
Angiography revealed that the stents restored blood flow in the vessel lumen and provided adequate coverage for dissection (Fig. 2B). A 3.0 mm×15 mm coronary balloon (Brosmed, Guangdong, China) was introduced from distal to proximal at the junction of V2 and V1 to the VA ostium along the microguidewire to dilate the occluded segment slowly. We retracted the balloon, a 4.0 mm×30 mm drug-eluting coronary stent (Medtronic, Minneapolis, USA) was implanted at the stenosis of V1, and a 4.5 mm×16 mm rapamycin drug-eluting vertebral stent (JWMS, Weihai, China) was implanted at the VA ostium. Angiography revealed that the stent was in a good position (Fig. 2C). The second angiography showed vascular stenosis at the junction of the V2 and V3 segments (Fig. 2D). A 2.5 × 10 mm intracranial balloon (Sinomed, Tianjin, China) was used to slowly dilate the stenosis, which improved the contrast (Fig. 2E).
After 10 min of observation, the left VA and basilar artery were clearly visible, with no residual stenosis or distant infarction of the vertebral basilar system. The mTICI grade was 3. (Fig. 2F).
Postoperative care
Following the operation, 6 ml (0.3 mg) of tirofiban was slowly added, and 6 ml (0.3 mg)/h of continuous pumping was applied for 1 day. The patient showed no further positive neurological indications, and no hemorrhage. At discharge, the patient had the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale [9] (NIHSS) score of 4 and the Modified Rankin Scale [10] (mRS) score of 2. Dual antiplatelet therapy and risk factor control techniques were required after discharge. Three months and 2 years after surgery, two DSAs indicated that the entire left VA segment was filled, and the mTICI grade was 3. (Fig. 3A, B). During the two-year follow-up, the patient reported no neurological problems.

Discussion
There are no standardized treatment criteria for symptomatic posterior circulation arterial occlusion beyond the time window [11–13]. Clinical studies have evaluated the efficacy and safety of endovascular recanalization for symptomatic nonacute VA occlusion [14], which suggests that nonacute intracranial arterial occlusion has a higher success rate of endovascular recanalization within 3 months of onset [15].
In this case, the patient’s left VA was dominant, and the right VA had clearly congenital thin in the intracranial region. DSA revealed that the posterior communicating artery was closed, and that the anterior circulation artery did not compensate for the posterior circulation. The imaging shows persistent cerebral hypoperfusion. In addition, the patient’s neurological deficits worsened despite intensive drug therapy. Imaging tests revealed persistent cerebral hypoperfusion and unconstructed collateral circulation. Moreover, preoperative DSA and HRMR-VWI revealed that the patient’s left VA in the V3 and V4 segments was blocked, the blood vessels were straight, and the overall shape was good. There was no ulcer in the blood vessel lumen or calcification in the blood vessel wall. Additionally, the occluded segment was a stable and soft plaque. Meanwhile, the V1 and V2 segments have low blockage times, and the distal vascular bed is good. The characteristic of this case was that after the patient’s acute occlusion of the intracranial artery stenosis, fresh thrombus gradually formed in the vascular lumen toward the proximal end, resulting in long-segment occlusion. The occluded segments covered the proximal full length of the VA (near V1 to V4) in 1 month. Timely intervention made it more likely for the microguidewire to pass through the incompletely organized thrombus in the vascular lumen [16]. In conclusion, early endovascular recanalization can effectively prevent small thrombi from migrating to the distal vasculature, thereby reducing the risk of scattered distal embolism and infarction. This not only inhibits the progression of infarct expansion but also alleviates neurological deficits and enhances cerebral perfusion.
Finally, we successfully established a new blood channel for the patient’s left VA. We found that a thorough preoperative evaluation was essential for such individuals. A detailed radiographic evaluation was vital in this process. Previously, due to a lack of effective evaluation of the length of the occluded segment, the nature of the occlusion, and the stability of the plaque, revascularization had a low success rate and significant surgical risks. Therefore, HRMR-VWI was essential before surgery. The use of dual-path angiography during this procedure increased its success rate. This technique allowed simultaneous visualization of both the proximal and distal blood vessels of the left VA occlusion segment. It also indicated the direction of the occlusion segment’s distal landing point, and determining the length of the occlusion segment. This allowed the microguidewire aid to pass smoothly through the subtotally occluded segment (the potential lumen) and the slow flow area first. Then, it passed through the anatomically occluded segment (the true occluded segment) to the distal arterial cavity of the V4 segment [14]. During the procedure, we noticed a vascular dissection in the VA. So, we carefully guided the microcatheter to make sure it was inside the true lumen of the distal V4 segment. Next, we slowly deployed the stent from the far end toward the near end right through the middle of the dissection. The stent fully covered the dissection area, which helps keep the clot from breaking loose and moving into the smaller blood vessels downstream. On the other hand, endovascular recanalization of long-segment nonacute VA occlusion is very risky, and whether it could be entend in such patients requires further studies.
Conclusion
Endovascular recanalization of symptomatic long-segment VA occlusion in the nonacute phase has traditionally been a high-risk surgery because of factors such as thrombus escape from the occluded vascular lumen and vascular morphology. This case demonstrates that revascularization is feasible for nonacute, long-segment VA occlusion after detailed preoperative evaluation and screening.
